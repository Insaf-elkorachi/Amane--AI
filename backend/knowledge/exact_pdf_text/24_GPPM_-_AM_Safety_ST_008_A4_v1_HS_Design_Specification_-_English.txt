Purpose 
 
This Health and Safety Design Specification provides standards for the design, modification, and 
installation of equipment, processes, and facilities at ArcelorMittal.  These standards are minimum 
requirements. The ArcelorMittal Asset Risk Management Standards are also to be used 
concurrently with this H&S Design Specification.  Where there is a Health and Safety Standard it will 
take priority over the Asset Risk Management Standard.  In all cases the national and local laws 
shall be complied with. 
 
Scope 
 
This document applies to all new and newly modified installations of equipment, processes and 
facilities at ArcelorMittal. Relevant information may be provided to vendors, construction 
contractors, and other internal ArcelorMittal functions. 
 
Responsibility 
 
The Vice President Health and Safety is responsible for preparing, issuing, and maintaining the 
Health and Safety Design Specification. The Corporate Health and Safety Department will perform 
the following activities with respect to the Specifications: 
 
Maintain awareness of developments related to Health and Safety Standards and 
technology that could require revisions to the Specification 
 
Request and coordinate feedback from users regarding proposed revisions and additions to 
the specifications 
 
Issue revisions to the specifications 
 
Control the electronic master of this document 
 
Method of Use 
 
The Project Manager shall use this document to prepare a “job specification” for each specific 
project, by extracting sections applicable to the scope of work to create a document that contains 
only the Health and Safety Design Specifications required for the job. 
 
Deviations 
 
Deviations from these specifications must be approved in writing by the Vice President, Health and 
Safety Department, or designate. A request for a deviation will be reviewed with all parties involved, 
and a consensus decision will be made. A deviation will be job-specific and will not apply to any 
other jobs unless specifically reviewed again and approved in writing. It is intended that deviations 
be kept to a minimum. 
 
Revisions 
 
Feedback regarding potential revisions to the Health and Safety Design Specification is encouraged 
on a continuing basis. Revisions will be issued on a regular basis, when warranted by changes in 
the legislation and/or ArcelorMittal standards and practices.

Section A 
 
 
1. Definitions 
 
“Shall”: When used to describe design requirements, the word “shall” indicates mandatory 
requirements, according to applicable legislation, regulations, and/or ArcelorMittal standards. 
 
“Should”: When used to describe design requirements, the word “should” indicates non-
mandatory requirements. However, these requirements are considered best practices. 
 
2. Standards and Codes 
 
In addition to standards outlined in this document, any equipment, processes and facilities 
supplied to ArcelorMittal shall comply, at minimum, with the Occupational Health and Safety 
Regulations that prevail in the country. 
 
A code is a law or regulation that sets forth minimum requirement and in particular, a building 
code is a law or regulation that sets forth minimum requirement for the design and construction 
of building and structures. These minimum requirements established to protect the health and 
safety of society, attempt to represent society’s compromise between optimum safety and 
economic feasibility.  
 
Standards and codes must be followed during the design. In countries, such as Brazil, United 
States, Canada, Australia, Western Europe, New Zealand, the design can follow the countries 
code and regulation. However, in less developed countries, the design must at the minimum 
follow the more stringent codes and regulations established in countries such as EU, United 
States or Canada. 
 
In this document, there are specifics referencing to guidelines from NFPA (National Fire 
Protection Association) and ANSI (American National Standard Institute). These are provided as 
world recognized standards, and shall be used as minimum requirement in safe design. 
 
Although ArcelorMittal will comply with specific minimum legal requirements of each country, the 
purpose of this Specification is to identify and incorporate best practice design standards into all 
of our operations.

In order to achieve these priorities, you must understand: 
 
The process that is being designed 
 
The level of risks associated with the process, equipment, layout, material, building, codes 
and regulation. 
 
Design with compromise between optimum health, safety and economic feasibility with 
an understanding that life safety shall never be compromised. 
 
General rules in safe designs are:      
 
Eliminate high risks at the design stage when possible 
 
Control the high risk when elimination not feasible 
 
Design effective and safe proof controls when hazard can not be eliminated: 
 
Apply safeguards and safety devices 
 
Allow for easy energy lock ability. Consider residual energies as storage banks of 
capacitors, hydraulic power, pressure of gases and fluids in general. 
 
Working at height elimination to the extent possible, and then sufficient and effective fall 
prevention devices when working at height can not be eliminated 
 
Confined spaces elimination or reduction 
 
Effective and dual (when necessary) machine guarding 
 
Effective ventilation system to protect the employee health and prevent fire and 
explosion 
 
Sufficient and effective fire detection and suppression system to protect life and property 
 
Chemical exposure control 
 
Health controls (avoid material used in building, process, equipment such as asbestos, 
lead, etc). 
 
Radiation 
 
Temperature fatigue 
 
Environmental impact 
 
Use Personal protective equipment (PPE) only as a last resort. 
 
Inputs from competent operations and maintenance personnel, who will ultimately be 
responsible for the process, are essential in recognizing and addressing hazards in day-
to-day work. 
 
Minimize direct employee handling of materials being processed. 
 
As much as possible, reduce the need for human interaction with physical, mechanical, 
and chemical hazards. This may be accomplished by increasing the level of automation 
for a task. 
 
Consider the consequences of increased automation: 
o Will the task now require the operator to control the equipment from a distance? 
o How will the operator see the task? 
o If cameras are provided, is there enough room in the pulpit to accommodate 
these monitors in an acceptable location? (Refer to design guidelines for pulpit 
layout for further information) 
 
Consider the tasks that operators and maintainers are required to perform on the 
equipment and processes. Are there hazards associated with the tasks? Can they be 
designed out of the equipment or process?

When designing a facility, emergency situations must be taken into account. 
Different types of emergencies depending on the location of the building being designed must 
be taken into consideration: 
 
 
Fire 
 
Explosion 
 
Earthquake 
 
Tornado 
 
Storm 
 
Flood 
 
Chemical releases 
 
Biological releases 
 
Bomb Threat 
 
Radioactivity 
 
2. Fundamentals of Safe Building and Process Design 
Following NFPA 550, a systematic approach to the principal fire safety strategies are: 
 
Prevent fire ignition 
 
Control combustion process 
 
Control fire by construction 
 
Detect fire early and provide notification 
 
Automatic suppression system 
 
Manual suppression system 
 
Manage houskeeping 
 
There are specific controls that can be chosen in each strategy selection process.   Below are 
some guidelines: 
 
Means of Egress 
The ability of building occupants to quickly and efficiently exit the building is often the 
difference between life and death. Model building codes have detailed provisions that 
address emergency egress. The means of egress that are needed depend primarily on 
the number of people who can occupy the space. Any building or structure design for 
human occupancy shall be provided with exits sufficient to permit the prompt escape of 
occupants in case of fire or other emergencies. The design of exits or other safe guards 
shall be such that reliance for safety to life in case of fire or other emergencies will not 
depend solely on any single safeguard. Additional safeguards shall be provided for life 
safety in case any single safeguard is ineffective due to some human or mechanical 
failure. 
 
Every exit shall be clearly visible or the route to reach it shall be conspicuously indicated 
in such a manner that every occupant of every building will readily know the direction of 
escape from any point, and each path of escape in its entirety shall be so arranged or

Emergency Eyewash Fountains and Showers 
Eyewash fountains shall be provided where there is risk of injury to the eyes. 
 
Eye/face wash fountains shall be provided where there is risk of injury to the eyes and 
face.  (When used in combination with a safety shower, it is not necessary to provide an

A safety shower shall be provided where there is risk of injury to the skin. 
 
Additionally, 
 
Water temperature shall be 21°C to 32°C (70°F to 90°F), with an optimum 
temperature of 27°C (80°F). 




the hazard and on the same level. 
 
Accessibility in Case of a Rescue 
The rescue may be from fall, confined spaces, chemical releases, or medical. 
The accessibility to each area for rescue should be part of the design.  
The responders, their vehicles and equipment should have sufficient space and 
accessibility for a timely rescue.

Depending on the location of the facility, the shelter in place for employees should also

3. Human Interaction, General Process Requirements (building design & layout) 
 
Process design shall minimize tripping hazards. Cables, cable trays, pipes, hoses and 
conduits shall be covered, preferably contained within a trench or elevated to pass over 
walkways. The electrical cables between trays and electric rooms must have active 
protection against spread of fire and smoke, also at the entrance to electrical panel. 
 
Provisions shall be made to capture fluids or other substances that may contribute to slips 
and falls. 
 
Pass line height, equipment location, and orientation should allow operations and 
maintenance personnel to perform work in upright postures, without excessive reaching, 
twisting or bending. 
 
Shutoff valves, controls, monitoring equipment, maintenance service apparatus and similar 
items shall be located at readily accessible floor or grade levels, to reduce the need to 
repetitively climb stairs, especially while carrying objects. 
 
Piping Identification - Process piping shall be identified and colour code as per the AM 
Technology Engineering Standards – Recommendations for Piping Identification. 
 
a. Vertical Clearance Requirements 
The minimum head clearance between the floor and a stationary “bump hazard” is 2.1m 
or 7 feet. If the hazard is significant (such as with a moving overhead crane), then this 
clearance shall be at least 3m or10 feet. 
 
b. Access to Equipment and Processes 
The following access methods are presented in order of preference: 
1. Permanent fixed stairs 
2. Vertical ladders 
 
Ship’s ladders are not permitted. (Ship’s ladders are also known as non-vertical ladders, 
which usually rises at an angle of 68° from the horizontal.) 
 
Stairs are preferable to ladders. Vertical ladders should be provided as a last resort, and 
only when the area is infrequently accessed. Walkways between elevated equipment 
should be provided in order to reduce the requirement to repeatedly climb stairs. 
 
Stairs to maintenance locations shall be provided; ladders shall be installed only after a 
risk assessment has deemed this acceptable. This risk assessment will consider the 
elevation difference, frequency of access, and tool requirements. 
 
The use of portable ladders, portable stairs, or powered elevating work platforms to 
access equipment or processes shall only be considered if absolutely necessary. 
Adequate clearance for these devices shall be provided. 
 
The facility and equipment shall be designed to avoid the requirement for maintenance 
or operations personnel to work from a ladder.

c. Fixed Ladders 
An access ladder in a fixed position shall: 
 
Be vertical 
 
Have rest platforms at no more than 9 metre intervals 
 
Be offset at each platform 
 
Where the ladder extends over 2.5 metres above ground, floor, or landing, have

 
Have rungs which are at least 15 cm from the wall and spaced at regular 
intervals. 
 
Have rungs that are a minimum of 41 cm or 16” wide. 
 
Ladders should be affixed to the platform such that the user can step forward, directly 
onto the platform, rather than sideways. 
 
 
d. Access To and Through the Area (Walkways) 
A safe access route to the equipment and through the area for operations and 
maintenance purposes shall be provided. 
 
 The following shall be considered when determining walkway location: 
 
 
Emergency egress and assembly areas 
 
Current pedestrian and equipment traffic patterns. 
 
Anticipated changes to the current patterns when new equipment is installed. Will 
traffic volume increase? Can it be conveniently rerouted? 
 
Location of lunch rooms, pulpits, meeting rooms, change houses, and parking 
lots. These are common destinations, and may affect compliance with 
designated walkways. 
 
Consider paths through buildings to adjacent areas  
 
Distance between equipment, chemical storage, chemical baths and walk ways 
 
Walkways shall be clearly distinguished from operational areas. This may be 
accomplished by the installation of guardrails to control pedestrian flow, or by painting 
areas on the floor. 
Walkways should generally be straight and convenient, with no unnecessary bends or 
detours. 
 
e. Width of Walkways 
Walkways should be a minimum of 92 cm or 36” wide indoors, and 153 cm or 60” wide 
outdoors. Allowance may need to be made for obstructions (such as doors) that protrude 
into the walkway.

5. Canteen 
 
a. Premises and Structures 
 
Care should be taken into designing ceiling heights, since low ceilings can lead to 
condensation and high ceilings are difficult to keep clean. 
 
Access to outside roofs and structures from inside the plant should be avoided, since 
roods often contain bird droppings that can be contaminated with Salmonella or other 
food-poisoning micro-organisms. 
 
Windows in processing areas should either be double-glazed or should be double 
windows to prevent condensation, and they should be mounted flush to the internal 
wall surface to avoid ledges. 
 
Shatterproof material should be used instead of glass in areas where broken glass 
could contaminate the food. 
 
Personnel entrances to processing areas should have two doors [that do not require 
use of the hands to open] with a lobby between the two doors.  The lobby should 
contain hand-washing facilities and storage facilities for clean protective clothing. 
 
The use of wall tiles is not recommended, since the area behind the tiles can serve 
as a breeding ground for insects if there is a failure of the tile grouting.  
 
There should be a path of width at least 1 metre around the area.  Trees, particularly 
deciduous trees, should be avoided, since rotting leaves can be a source of mould 
and the trees can give harbourage to birds. 
 
b. Storage, Distribution and Transportation  
The storage, distribution and transportation of foodstuffs shall be such as to prevent 
damage, contamination or deterioration of the foodstuffs or of materials that come into 
contact with it. 
 
 
Specific Requirements – Storage 
 
There shall be adequate storage facilities for raw materials, in-process materials, 
where applicable, and finished product. 
 
Unprocessed food shall not be stored in the same store as processed food, 
unless it can be shown that there is no risk of cross-contamination.  Raw, 
unprocessed food shall be effectively separated from processed food, either 
physically or by time. 
 
Stores shall be proofed against rodents, insects and birds, and shall be kept in a 
hygienic condition. 
 
There shall be a passageway of width at least 0.5m around the walls or 
partitions of all stores, to preclude the establishment of breeding sites for 
rodents, to enable cleaning (e.g. vacuum cleaning) and to aid hygienic 
inspections.  Where racks are used against walls, there shall be a clearance of 
at least 0.3m between the floor and the bottom of the rack.

The machine design and construction, in particular electric, hydraulic and pneumatic equipment





a. Selection of Safeguards 
The choice of safeguarding methodology shall be in the following order of priority: 
 
Eliminate or reduce the hazard (usually through changing the machine design) 
 
Eliminate or change the task (consider providing a tool to eliminate the need to 
access the danger zone, change the location of the person to outside the danger 
zone when they perform the task, add automation to eliminate the task),  
 
Apply safeguarding technology (devices, circuits and engineering controls) to 
reduce the likelihood of harm (barriers, interlocks, etc.) 
 
Apply awareness means and warnings 
 
Develop and apply procedures and training (administrative controls) 
 
Use Personal Protective Equipment

to the equipment for the purposes of operations and maintenance activities, such as 
visual inspection, lubrication, testing and trouble shooting, and maintenance activities

awareness methods, which do not provide sufficient personnel protection against 
machine hazards.

2. Control of Hazardous Energy (Isolations and Interrupts) 
 
There are two primary ways to control hazardous energy when access to the danger zone of 
equipment is required: 
 
a) Solution: uses a mechanical energy isolation device to physically prevent or restrain the 
energy flow (e.g. circuit breaker, disconnect switch, valve, block or pin). A lock is applied 
to the energy isolating device. 
 
b) Control Interrupt: uses control power to interrupt the flow of energy to a process, 
equipment, machine or device.  Control power typically prevents the energy isolating 
device from operating.

9. Ventilation System 
 
In many cases ventilation system is the best way of keeping contaminants down to a safe level. 
Two types of exhaust ventilation system exist: General and Local. 
General Dilution works by supplying outside air to be mixed with the contaminants to safe 
levels.  
 
Local Exhaust systems work by capturing the contaminants at its point of generation and 
exhausting it outdoors (example: fume hood). This method is far more effective at removing 
airborne contaminants from the source and should always be used unless it is impractical for 
the work area. A combination of both systems may be necessary for the work area.  
All Ventilation systems should be engineer-designed. The following points should be noted 
when choosing a new ventilation system. 
 
Local Exhaust systems are preferred over general dilution. They allow less contaminant to be 
released into the work area. General dilution should only be used when local exhaust system 
are not practical. Also operational costs of dilution systems are usually much higher.  
Ventilation systems should be evaluated frequently to ensure their effectiveness. 
 
a. General Dilution 
 
Exhaust intakes should be placed near the point of contamination if possible air 
supply and exhaust systems should be placed so that air flow moves from the 
supply, past the worker, through the zone of contamination and into the exhaust. 
This orientation will reduce the amount of chemicals that can enter the worker’s 
breathing zone.  
 
Outlet discharging exhaust should be far away from any outside air intake or 
windows. Exhaust should be discharged high above the roof line. 
 
The recirculation for the contaminated air must be prevented. 
 
The amount of chemical being released and its toxicity should be considered when 
establishing adequate air flow rate ventilation. 
 
b. Local Exhaust System 
 
A contaminant’s point of entry into a local exhaust system is known as a hood. Two 
types of hoods exist: enclosing and exterior. Enclosing hood systems completely or 
partially enclose the process or source of contamination. Exterior hoods are located 
adjacent to the source of contamination and do not enclose it. Enclosing hoods are 
more effective at removing contaminant, and should be used whenever feasible. 
Enclose a process releasing hazardous chemicals as much as possible. 


3. Storage of Gases 
 
Fire protection safe guards for gas storage reflect (1) the hazards of the container/gas 
combination and (2) the hazards of the gas when it escapes from the container. Discussion 
of basic hazards of the gases, gas emergency control, and specific gases are especially 
pertinent to the safe storage of gases and must be evaluated and assessed prior to the 
design of the storage area and layout. The containers must be gas tight for both the range of 
temperature and pressure conditions present at the storage site as well as condition of 
transportation environment prior to arrival there. 
  
4. Compressed Gas Storage, Handling and Transportation 
 
Compressed gas cylinders are used in many workplaces to store gases that vary from 
extremely flammable (acetylene) to extremely inert (helium). Many compressed gas cylinders 
are stored at extremely high pressures (up to 2,500 pounds per square inch gauge or PSIG). A 
sudden release of these gases can cause a cylinder to become a missile-like projectile. 
Cylinders have been known to penetrate concrete-block walls. If handled properly compressed 
gas cylinders are safe. If handled improperly, the same cylinders can present a severe hazard 
to you and the surrounding area. 
  
Types of Hazards: 
 
Physical Damage: Cylinders are very hazardous when exposed to damage from falling 
over, tipping, heat, electric circuits, motion, vibration, or anything that can cause a weakness 
or crack in the cylinder wall or shell. Such damage can cause the cylinder to rupture and 
explode sending sharp metal pieces, like shrapnel, blasting through the area.  
 
Tipping and Falling: The most common hazard associated with cylinders occurs when 
cylinders tip or fall over. Since cylinders are heavy and awkward to handle, they require 
special care and equipment in handling and securing so they do not fall or tip over and 
cause injury.  
 
Valve Leakage: Cylinder valves can leak, causing their contents to discharge. To minimize 
hazards from leaks, use proper ventilation and storage.

7. Storage of Chemicals 
 
The first principal of good storage of chemicals is segregation including separation from other 
materials in storage, from processing and handling operation, and from incompatible materials. 
Segregation can take the form of isolation in a separate detached structure or building. 
Segregation can also be achieved by separating chemicals within the same building by an 
intervening empty area or by intervening storage of inert or non hazardous materials. The extent 
of segregation depends on the quantity of the materials being stored, the physical state of the 
chemicals and their degree of in-compatibility.  
In addition, the known behaviour of materials under fire condition will affect the extent and type 
of segregation needed. Rupture of vessels and possible mixing of incompatible materials under 
fire condition must be considered.

Before selecting the chemical, evaluate information about its toxicity. When the toxicity

c. Oxidizing Chemicals 
The oxidizers shall not be stored in the same storage area as flammable and combustible 
liquids. Storage building shall be of non-combustible or fire-resistance construction. 
Combustible packaging and wood or plastic pallets represent a sever hazard and shall be 
eliminated. Because certain oxidizing materials undergo dangerous reactions with specific 
non-combustible materials, the possibility of dangerous reactions shall be considered when 
designing storage areas. Chlorates shall not be stored with acids or combustible materials. 
Inorganic peroxides also react with acids yielding hydrogen peroxide. A suitable storage 
facility for inorganic peroxides would be a dry fire resistance storage room in which there are 
no combustible or acids.  
 
d. Combustible Chemicals 
Essentially all organic chemicals are combustible. Storage practices for such chemicals 
closely follow those for the more common solid combustible materials.  
 
e. Radioactive material 
Radioactive elements and compounds have fire and explosion hazards identified to those of 
the same material when not radioactive. An additional hazard is introduced by the various 
types of radiation emitted all of which are capable of causing damage to living tissues. 
Under fire conditions, vapour and dust can be formed that could contaminate the building of 
origin, but also neighbouring buildings and outdoor areas. The main concern is to prevent 
the release or loss of control of those materials by fire during fire extinguishing.

Maintenance of Building and Equipment 
  
1. Access Requirements 
 
Provide adequate access routes for maintenance personnel to safely access their working 
areas.  Locate shutoff valves, controls, monitoring equipment, maintenance service apparatus 
and similar items at readily accessible floor or grade levels. 
 
The ability to maintain equipment is equally as important as the functioning of the equipment.  
Equipment shall be designed to allow for safe and efficient maintenance.  Designing for 
accessibility includes visual access as well as reach access.  The maintainer must be able to 
see their work, and must be able to reach it with the appropriate tool or their hands, as required.  
The most common practice is to include ports or spaces to allow clearance during maintenance 
tasks.



kg) 
 
Locate items to be maintained between shoulder and waist height, approximately 81 – 
142cm (32 – 56”) from the standing surface

 
Design safe access to roof, attic area, tanks, boiler and engine rooms 
 
Design appropriate escape routs in case of the emergency 

