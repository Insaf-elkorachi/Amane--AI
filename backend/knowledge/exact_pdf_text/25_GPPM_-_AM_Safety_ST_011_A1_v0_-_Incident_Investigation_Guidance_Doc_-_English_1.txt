1. Introduction 
 
Incident investigation is a key element of a Health and Safety Program. The purpose of this guidance 
document is to assist users in the development of a consistent and effective incident investigation process 
with the goal to prevent incident recurrence. The incident investigation process involves the following four 
interrelated activities: collect incident data, describe incident sequence, determine causal factors, and 
develop preventive and corrective actions. 
 
2. What Is An Incident? 
 
An incident is an unplanned event that may or may not result in an injury or damage. Incidents include 
near misses. 
 
An incident without injury or a near miss are opportunities to fix something before someone is injured, or 
further damage or loss is experienced.  
 
Incidents don't just happen “by accident”. They may occur when: 
 
People work in an unsafe manner; and/or, 
 
Unsafe work conditions exist. 
 
When an incident happens, it must be reported immediately so that an investigation can be directly 
undertaken. 
 
3. Why Do We Investigate Incidents? 
 
No incident should ever be treated as “acceptable”. Therefore, all incidents must be investigated. 
 
A thorough investigation leads to a better understanding of the causal factors. The causal factors are used 
to identify work-related problems and deficiencies, and provide the bases for effective solutions that will 
prevent further incidents and injuries. Incident prevention involves helping people work safely and 
maintaining safe working conditions at all times.  
 
4. How Do We Investigate? 
 
The incident investigation process involves four interrelated activities: collect incident data, describe 
incident sequence, determine causal factors, and develop preventive and corrective actions. Although each 
activity provides an important foundation for the activities which follow, the investigation efforts can often 
go back and forth in an iterative fashion, eg., in describing the incident sequence, more questions may 
arise therefore requiring the collection of additional incident data.  
 
 
Collect Incident Data 
 
The first step in the investigative process is the collection of work-related data pertaining to the relevant 
events and circumstances involved in the incident. This may include information on personnel, tasks, 
equipment and environmental conditions. This data collection is important as it provides the foundation for 
the following investigative activities. 
 
Acting as the lead investigator, incident investigations are normally organized and conducted by the 
Immediate Supervisor of where the incident occurred. 
 
For fatality and significant incidents, site should involved senior managers from other ArcelorMittal 
sites to support the investigation process.

The incident investigation should start as soon as possible with a visit to the incident site: 
 
as memories are still fresh; 
 
while the site has not been disturbed; and, 
 
before another similar incident can occur.  
However, the investigation should begin only after the incident scene is safe and any injured persons have 
been attended to. If the incident scene could potentially pose a further danger, immediate action must be 
taken to limit further impact by isolating the scene of the incident while ensuring anything that might be 
considered evidence or information is not damaged, disturbed, or removed from the scene.  
 
The following information, as a minimum, should be collected: 
 
Date and Time of Incident – including date and time when reported (if different) 
 
Location: Business Unit/Department, Sub-department plus the specific location within that 
department (e.g., building, line, furnace, column number, roadway, aisle, name of equipment) 
 
Employee Information (if injury): Name, employee number, regular job code, job code at time of 
incident (if different), job start dates 
 
Supervisor Information: Name, employee number, job title 
 
Injury Information: Type of injury and body part injured 
 
Details of any equipment involved including types, model numbers, ID numbers, size, weight and 
other measurements. In some cases, photographs, video, etc may be required to preserve the 
evidence.  
 
Pertinent records – training records, written job procedures (SOPs), work instructions, job safe 
practices, H&S meeting minutes, H&S communications, contractor documentation. 
 
All significant information and findings should be validated with supporting fact-based evidence.  
 
Depending on the incident, the following people should be considered for interviews, as their input could 
provide valuable information to the investigation: 
 
Injured employee, if available 
 
Witnesses 
 
Area Supervision 
 
Other Employees currently performing the same work or located in the same work area; 
 
Persons who can contribute because of their knowledge of equipment, procedures or past 
experience (e.g., Trainers, Maintenance, Engineering, Security) 
 
Health and Safety Department staff 
 
Health and Safety Committee Representatives – as required 
 
People should be interviewed separately to avoid one person being influenced by another. It is advisable to 
take written statements, especially in an investigation dealing with serious injury (or serious potential). In 
these cases, the injured employee and other key witnesses should be asked to provide written statements 
concerning the sequence of events which led to the incident. These statements can be hand written by the 
employee or written by another employee.  
 
Consideration should also be given to holding the investigation, and especially interviews, in a quiet and 
controlled location. In order to gather as much information and detail as possible, it is recommended that 
other people be assigned to support the lead investigator in the investigation process (eg., providing 
assistance in taking notes).

For employee statements, in addition to ensuring a full description and explanation of all events leading to 
the incident is provided, the following types of questions should be considered for the injured/affected 
employee(s): 
 
Was this the normal job? 
 
What task was being completed at the time of the incident? 
 
What was the plan for the work (job and/or task)? 
 
Had anything changed in the job or itself? 
 
Had there been any change in the work process, or any of the inputs or outputs? 
 
Since incidents do not always result from a specific or single event, were there significant physical, 
cognitive and/or environmental job demands over time? 
 
Were concerns raised to others?  
 
 
Describe Incident Sequence 
 
A re-construction of the incident sequence can be described by arranging the relevant events and 
circumstances into a time-based sequence. This can be a complicated and time demanding activity, 
especially if the data collection is less than adequate or overwhelming. As well, the data may appear at 
times to be conflicting or even contradictory. Convening a group to review the incident data and the 
relative timings of events can help in defining the most probable reconstruction of the incident sequence.  
 
 
Determine Causal Factors 
 
Incidents involve a sequence of events or circumstances that occur during the course of work activity, or 
prior to its initiation, and culminate in the unplanned event. Within the sequence of events and 
circumstances are unsafe conditions or actions that are considered to be causative factors. 
 
A key to analyzing the causes of the incident is to work backwards through the sequence of events and 
keep asking the question “Why?” until all causes are identified. 
 
Causal analysis is not about assigning blame for the incident. Blame tends to focus on a single cause; 
whereas prevention recognizes that there are multiple causes. For the vast majority of incidents the causes 
tend to be management system failures. It is important to determine the exact causes, or at least most 
probable ones, so that effective preventive and correct actions can be developed. 
 
4.3.1 
Root Cause Analysis and Critical Decision Analysis (RCA/CDA) 
 
Root cause analysis involves the charting of the events and causal factors in an incident sequence. For 
every effect there are causes. Getting to the root of an issue is the process of identifying these causes. 
There is usually not one cause, but rather a system of causes.  Taking the root cause analysis technique 
further, a “Critical Decision Analysis” allows us to understand the motivation or reasoning behind any 
‘decisions’ or actions that were made that contributed to the incident (effect).  
 
Whenever the root cause analysis indicates that a ‘critical decision’ was made by someone, it is important 
to ask the question “What was the person planning to do?” This helps to create an   understanding of the 
type of decision or action that caused the effect, and lead to a further analysis of the underlying 
behavioural antecedent factors. 
 
For further information on the RCA/CDA approach, refer to the Root Cause Analysis/Critical Decision 
Analysis: Guidance Document.

4.4 Developing Preventive and Corrective Actions 
 
The decision regarding what action should be taken to prevent similar incidents from happening again and 
correct any problems must be based on the causal factors. It may not be effective to just address an 
individual problem or concern (eg., by ‘reinstructing’ one employee). It may be necessary to consider all 
situations where a similar incident could occur and apply preventive actions to those situations as well. 
 
A specific action plan should be developed in order to ensure:  
 
Both immediate and longer-term solutions are considered 
 
“Who” is responsible for a given action is clearly identified and understood 
 
Expectations for completion dates are understood 
 
Actions are completed 
 
Follow-up occurs to determine that the solutions are working properly and maintained as intended 
If persons conducting the investigation do not have the authority to initiate some actions, senior 
supervision should be contacted as soon as reasonably possible so they can decide on appropriate action. 
 
5. Documentation of Incident Investigation 
 
The findings of the incident investigation, including the relevant incident data, the incident sequence, the 
root causal factors and the preventive and corrective actions, should be documented. Records of these 
investigations should be maintained in a way to permit easy access to this information for use in: 
 
Comparative assessments with other incidents 
 
Trend and statistical analyses 
 
Audits of the effective application and closure of the incident investigation process 
 
If the incident and the actions being taken have any application to other Business Areas/departments or 
areas and they have not been involved in the investigation to-date, a copy of the investigation should be 
shared with the other Business Areas/departments/areas as soon as possible.
